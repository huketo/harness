# Narrative and Delivery Logic

Use this reference when the deck's argument, sequence, speaker notes, or adaptation of existing material needs design. It owns narrative decisions, not rendering syntax or visual styling.

## Anchor the audience outcome

Write one audience contract before outlining slides:

- **Audience:** who will use the deck and what they already know.
- **Outcome:** what they should understand, decide, approve, try, or remember afterward.
- **Constraint:** time, setting, language, sensitivity, and required deliverables.
- **Success signal:** an observable response, such as choosing an option, explaining a mechanism, or completing an exercise.

Phrase the outcome as a change in the audience, not as “cover these topics.” Resolve competing outcomes by naming one primary outcome and assigning supporting outcomes to specific sections or companion material.

**Done when:** every required topic either advances the primary outcome or is explicitly retained for compliance, reference, or handoff.

## Choose the consumption mode

Treat the mode as an editorial contract:

| Mode | The slide must carry | The notes or presenter carry |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `presentation` | A legible focal message and the evidence needed in the room | Context, nuance, transitions, delivery cues, and optional detail |
| `reading` | Context, definitions, captions, evidence, sources, and conclusions sufficient without narration | Authoring notes only; no essential explanation |

When both modes are required, keep one editable source and derive two outputs or views from it. Mark which material appears only in presenter view, only in the reading version, or in both. Do not maintain two unrelated manual decks that can drift.

A presentation deck may use restraint and staged disclosure. A reading deck may need denser explanations. Neither mode has a universal word count, slide count, or seconds-per-slide threshold; density follows content, language, accessibility, and setting.

**Done when:** removing the presenter from the reading version loses no required meaning, while the presentation version remains support for speech rather than a transcript of it.

## Shape the story around the purpose

Select a structure that matches the audience's job, then alter it as evidence demands:

- **Decision or approval:** decision context → criteria → viable options → consequences → recommendation → requested decision.
- **Technical explanation:** motivating behavior → system model → mechanism → evidence or demonstration → limitations → practical use.
- **Teaching or workshop:** learner goal → prerequisite model → worked example → guided practice → check for understanding → transfer task.
- **Research or analysis:** question → method and boundaries → observations → interpretation → implications → uncertainties.
- **Status or incident review:** expected state → observed state → impact → contributing conditions → response → current risk and next action.

These are working shapes, not mandatory pitch arcs. Add an agenda when navigation helps, a recap when retention matters, and a question slide when discussion is part of the outcome. Omit ceremonial sections that do no work.

Read the sequence in titles only. The titles should expose the logic: setup, turn, evidence, and conclusion. A title may be a claim when the body supports a claim; covers, dividers, exercises, and navigation slides can use functional labels.

**Done when:** a reviewer can explain why each slide appears where it does and how the final section fulfills the audience contract.

## Bind claims to evidence

For each consequential claim, identify what would make it credible to this audience. Use the most direct available support: user-provided facts, primary sources, local code, measured results, dated records, or clearly bounded expert reasoning.

Record provenance close enough to survive export:

- identify the source or local artifact precisely enough to retrieve it;
- retain dates, versions, units, populations, and comparison conditions that affect meaning;
- distinguish quoted fact, measurement, interpretation, recommendation, and example;
- state uncertainty or missing evidence instead of filling the gap with plausible detail;
- use screenshots to show the depicted state, not as proof of an unshown cause or outcome;
- label synthetic values and mock screens as illustrative, never as measured evidence.

Prefer a visible caption or compact source line when readers need to judge the evidence. Put longer citations and retrieval notes in presenter notes or the reading appendix only when the visible claim remains assessable.

**Done when:** every decision-driving claim can be traced to a source, a disclosed reasoning step, or an explicit “unverified” boundary.

## Build the storyboard record

Create one row or block per slide with these fields:

- **Title:** the on-slide title or functional label.
- **Purpose:** the change this slide should cause in the audience.
- **Evidence:** the source, artifact, example, or reasoning that supports it.
- **Visual format:** comparison, chart, code, diagram, image, timeline, table, or deliberate text.
- **Notes:** explanation, caveat, interaction cue, and delivery intent.
- **Time:** a relative or approximate budget justified by the speaking task.

Add dependencies when a slide needs a definition, prior reveal, live demo, or audience response. Mark optional slides and appendix material so time pressure has a planned cut path rather than an improvised omission of required reasoning.

Balance the deck by effort, not by equal slide duration. A diagram explanation or exercise may need several minutes; a divider may take seconds. If the time budget fails, remove repetition, move reference detail, shorten interaction, or split the deliverable before shrinking essential evidence beyond legibility.

**Done when:** all required content, sources, interactions, and conclusions have a home, and the planned cut path preserves the primary outcome.

## Write notes and transitions

For each substantive presentation slide, write notes that add what the screen should not carry:

- the point to say first;
- the explanation or story that earns the conclusion;
- caveats, definitions, pronunciation, and source context;
- the intended audience cue or interaction;
- the transition that creates a reason for the next slide;
- the time or pacing cue when timing matters.

Write transitions as logical handoffs, not stage directions alone: “We have established the failure mode; now compare the two mitigations” is useful, while “next slide” is not. Keep private or sensitive notes out of public exports and handouts.

Rehearse or reason through the spoken path using the actual notes. Adjust the storyboard where the explanation depends on material that is absent, arrives too late, or is repeated.

**Done when:** the presenter can deliver the argument from the notes without inventing missing logic, and the transitions make the sequence feel necessary.

## Preserve meaning in complex or existing material

Before revising, converting, or combining sources, inventory:

- required claims, figures, quotations, terminology, and legal or compliance language;
- audience promises, decisions, and calls to action;
- ordering dependencies and references between slides;
- speaker-note content and hidden context;
- contradictions, obsolete material, and genuinely optional detail.

Create a simple mapping from each required source item to its destination slide, appendix, notes, or an explicitly approved omission. Preserve the existing engine and editorial intent unless the request requires a change. Reorganize for clarity, but do not silently weaken claims, drop qualifiers, change measured values, or replace original evidence with generic decoration.

For compound requests, separate the obligations: for example, “shorten, translate, and convert” requires semantic preservation, language accuracy, and format delivery. A polished conversion is incomplete if any obligation disappears.

**Done when:** every required source item is preserved or intentionally dispositioned, all changed claims remain equivalent or are called out, and no unresolved omission is hidden by the new design.

## Narrative completion gate

Narrative work is complete only when:

- the audience contract and consumption mode are explicit;
- the chosen structure fits the purpose rather than a generic pitch formula;
- consequential claims have honest, recoverable provenance;
- the storyboard contains title, purpose, evidence, visual format, notes, and time;
- notes, transitions, interaction cues, and cut paths support actual delivery;
- reading outputs stand alone when requested;
- adaptations preserve required meaning and expose approved omissions;
- synthetic numbers, mock screens, and screenshots are labeled for what they actually demonstrate.
