## Positioning and strategy basics

### Write the strategic frame

Collect only missing inputs: audience and buying trigger; alternatives; product and delivery status; revenue model; funnel evidence; goal and horizon; constraints; prior efforts. Budget and team details are optional unless the requested plan commits resources.

1. Define the audience by its situation, job, and constraints, not demographics alone. Separate user, buyer, approver, and beneficiary where they differ.
2. Name the job and triggering event: what changes that makes the audience seek help now?
3. List real alternatives, including manual work, doing nothing, and adjacent solutions. Compare on criteria buyers use.
4. State the category in language the audience understands. Category creation needs an explanation and evidence, not just a new label.
5. Select a differentiated benefit, its mechanism, and supporting proof. Make trade-offs explicit: who is served best and who is outside the focus?
6. Define the business-model thesis: how customer value becomes sustainable revenue or participation, and what repeat behavior matters.
7. Choose a small set of strategic bets. Each bet names the audience, mechanism, advantage, constraint, and measurable outcome.

A positioning draft can follow: “For [audience in situation], [offering] is a [category] that helps [job/outcome] through [distinct mechanism], supported by [evidence], unlike [alternative and relevant trade-off].” Treat unsupported parts as hypotheses, not launch-ready claims.

### Test market quality and reachability

Assess problem severity and frequency together. Frequent problems may support habit-based retention; rare but important problems may depend on timing, trust, or repeat demand from new buyers. Low severity constrains willingness to pay even when usage is frequent. These are planning hypotheses to validate, not a universal ranking of markets.

When sizing changes the decision, define geography, buyer, use case, pricing model, and exclusions first:

- **Total addressable market (TAM):** the defined category's possible annual spend.
- **Serviceable addressable market (SAM):** the part the current offering can serve.
- **Serviceable obtainable market (SOM):** the share reachable within the plan horizon, given channels, capacity, competition, and economics.

Triangulate at least two relevant methods: count reachable buyers and annual spend; model intent-led search demand; inspect verified competitor activity; or model channel reach under actual capacity. Keep units, geography, and time periods comparable. Distinguish search demand from latent demand, and observed competitor facts from inferred revenue.

Show ranges, source dates, confidence, and sensitivity to major assumptions. Reconcile disagreements before using the estimate to justify spending. A large TAM alone is not evidence of willingness to buy.

### Treat strategy as resource allocation

Balance proven activity, the next plausible growth source, and bounded experiments. Choose the mix from runway, learning needs, and existing evidence rather than a fixed percentage rule.

Prefer assets with reuse: one customer story can inform sales, search content, onboarding, and social. Account for production and maintenance costs; “organic” is not cost-free.

Plan for plateaus in channels, products, and segments. Name the evidence that would trigger another channel or segment, and the lead time needed to prepare it. Forecasts are scenarios, not promises of smooth exponential growth.

## Marketing plan procedure

AARRR means Acquisition, Activation, Retention, Referral, and Revenue. It structures diagnosis and ownership; it is not a mandatory chronological customer journey. Commerce may collect revenue before activation, and marketplaces need a funnel for each side.

### 1. Assess the current state

Reuse a dated existing assessment when relevant; update only areas with changed evidence. Capture what is live, completed, in progress, paused, or blocked so the plan builds on actual work.

Assess the following surfaces where applicable:

| Area group | Surfaces to inspect |
|---|---|
| Strategy | Positioning, customer research, messaging |
| Decision surfaces | Homepage or primary listing, product/sales pages, campaign pages, comparisons, pricing |
| Value delivery | Onboarding, lifecycle communication, support/resource content |
| Distribution | Launches, paid acquisition, search, localization |
| Operating discipline | Sales materials, conversion testing and instrumentation |

For each surface record evidence/date, maturity, stage affected, and the decision it changes. Use an ordinal rubric only when helpful: 0 = confirmed absent; 1 = ad hoc; 2 = partial; 3 = consistent; 4 = measured and maintained; 5 = repeatedly improved with evidence. Mark missing evidence **unknown**, and irrelevant surfaces **N/A**. A low sales-material score need not matter to a self-serve consumer product.

Interpret the pattern, not a grand total. A polished homepage cannot compensate for a broken first-use experience. End with the strongest assets, the limiting constraint, and the highest-impact uncertainty.

### 2. Identify the AARRR constraint

| Stage | Decision question | Useful measures | Priority condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acquisition | Can the right people discover and consider us? | Qualified visits, inquiries, listing-to-install, acquisition cost | Too little qualified demand to learn or grow |
| Activation | Do newcomers reach a first meaningful outcome? | Completion of value event, time-to-value, setup abandonment | Interest exists but first-use value is weak |
| Retention | Do people return when the job recurs? | Cohort retention, repeat purchase, renewal, churn reasons | First value works but repeat value fails |
| Referral | Do satisfied people bring relevant newcomers? | Referred activated users, referral completion, incentive cost | Value is proven and sharing fits the context |
| Revenue | Does pricing and delivery support viable economics? | Contribution margin, paid conversion, expansion, payback | Demand exists but monetization or costs constrain viability |

Define boundaries once: signup-page intent belongs to acquisition; signup completion belongs to activation; first meaningful value is a separate activation milestone. Referral-origin customers still pass through acquisition and activation. Brand and content support several stages rather than forming a sixth stage.

Select one or two constraints from cohort evidence and customer explanations. With no users, seek a small qualified audience and test value. With churn, prioritize repeat value before expanding volume. When the funnel works at small scale, test incremental acquisition economics before scaling. Sparse data calls for research or a bounded learning experiment.

### 3. Set goals and assumptions

For each goal state baseline, target or target range, deadline, population, owner, and evidence. Separate business outcomes from leading indicators and delivery milestones.

Tie the near-term roadmap to the longer-term outcome. Mark unresolved assumptions and the observation that would invalidate each. Estimate downside, working, and upside scenarios when budget or forecasts are requested.

Use model-specific goals: qualified pipeline for sales-assisted buying; retained value-achieving users for an app; successful matches and repeat transactions for a marketplace; repeat engaged members for a community. Counts such as followers or downloads matter only with a downstream mechanism.

### 4. Select channels for stage and capability

| Situation | Candidate focus | Gate before expansion |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-launch or little demand evidence | Customer-language research, targeted discovery, early-access preparation | Audience fit and a deliverable first-value experience |
| Early traction with limited resources | One credible distribution channel, reusable content, onboarding and lifecycle foundations | Repeatable quality signals and owner capacity |
| Proven value, uncertain distribution | Bounded channel tests, useful partnerships, intent capture | Reliable measurement, costs, and downstream quality |
| Repeatable economics and delivery | Incremental channel scaling, new formats or segments | Marginal economics, support capacity, and saturation checks |
| Expansion to geography or audience | Local research, localized offer and distribution | Product/policy readiness and regional economics |

Business variants change the emphasis, not the planning standard: B2B may need evaluation material and a sales handoff; consumer apps need listing and first-session coherence; creators and communities need trust and ongoing participation; marketplaces need side-specific activation and liquidity; physical products need inventory and fulfillment capacity.

For each candidate explain audience access, buyer intent, creative or expertise requirements, time-to-signal, resource cost, and risk. Label channels **active**, **test now**, **later with named prerequisite**, or **skip with reason**. Paid testing can be appropriate early if affordable and informative; scaling requires healthy downstream value and economics, not an arbitrary funding label or organic-first rule.

Prepare the draft/checklist for campaigns, outreach, publishing, events, or spending; execution needs explicit authorization naming the action (see SKILL.md).

### 5. Add budget and team only when relevant

Keep a currently executable plan separate from a contingent plan. Funding, additional capacity, or a product release is an unlock only when its trigger is explicit; projected funds are not available funds.

Two budgeting paths:

- **Capacity-led:** start with the approved spend and time ceiling, then estimate achievable outcomes using measured economics.
- **Goal-led:** estimate needed net new customers or transactions after retained revenue, churn, and expansion; work backward through conversion stages and fully loaded acquisition costs.

Include people, production, distribution, events, software, delivery dependencies, and maintenance. Define which sales costs belong in customer acquisition cost (CAC), use consistent periods, and allow for the acquisition-to-payment lag. Separate channel costs from blended CAC. Use ranges when economics are unknown rather than treating a plan price as an acquisition-cost benchmark.

Check contribution margin, payback, cash timing, refund exposure, inventory or service capacity, and the opportunity cost of founder time. Allocate an explicit bounded learning reserve suited to uncertainty; choose it rather than importing a generic spend ratio.

Keep accountable ownership of strategy, customer knowledge, and claims with the project. Specialist execution can be external when scope, acceptance criteria, access, and review responsibility are clear. Assign owners for distribution/experiments, positioning/offers, and content/trust; one person may own several. Hire or contract against the limiting capability, not a prescribed title.

Map operations by capability rather than vendors: content production, lifecycle preparation, event definitions, attribution, commercial records, and reporting. Show existing capacity, gaps, owner, handoffs, and unlock condition.

### 6. Define measurement and review hooks

Choose a primary outcome that reflects delivered customer value and business viability. Pair it with guardrails so revenue growth cannot hide churn, poor quality, or harmful user outcomes.

For each metric define event or calculation, denominator, cohort, window, source, owner, baseline, and review threshold. Include a leading indicator per priority AARRR stage. Attribute customers consistently and avoid double-counting multi-channel influence. Use [measurement](measurement.md) for implementation and experiment rigor.

Prepare the draft/checklist for tracking changes; execution needs explicit authorization naming the action (see SKILL.md).

Set a cadence appropriate to volume and decision speed: operational review for work and blockers; periodic cohort/economics review for continue/change/stop decisions; strategic recalibration for goals, capacity unlocks, and channel saturation. Specify the observation window needed before judging slow channels.

### 7. Sequence and synthesize

Sequence prerequisite fixes, foundational assets, bounded experiments, then expansion. Every initiative needs an owner, deliverable, dependency, cost or effort, stage, success signal, and decision date.

Use a near-term roadmap with concrete work and a longer outlook with conditional milestones. Draft the executive summary last. Ensure the chosen bets, budget, owners, metrics, and roadmap agree; expose unresolved decisions instead of assuming approval.

### Marketing plan output template

Adapt the horizon to the request; a 90-day roadmap and 12-month outlook are useful defaults, not fixed commitments.

| Plan section | Required contents |
|---|---|
| Executive summary | Priority constraint; major bets; near-term priorities; intended outcome; key uncertainty |
| Strategic frame | Audience/job/trigger; alternatives; differentiation/proof; business model; voice; exclusions |
| Current state | Dated evidence; surface assessment; prior and in-flight work; blockers; strengths/gaps |
| Acquisition | Current channels; chosen/test/later/skip decisions; audience mechanism; measures |
| Activation | First-value event; friction hypotheses; delivery/onboarding moves; measures |
| Retention | Recurring job; cohort behavior; churn causes; repeat-value/lifecycle moves; measures |
| Referral | Natural sharing moment; eligible audience; incentive and abuse considerations; measures |
| Revenue | Actual pricing/policy; packaging or offer hypotheses; margins/payback; measures |
| Near-term roadmap | Initiative, stage, owner, timing, dependency, resource need, signal, decision date |
| Longer-term outlook | Conditional milestones; next channel/segment; capacity triggers; scenarios |
| Operations and optional resources | Capability/owner/handoff map; approved capacity; contingent budget/team |
| Prioritized idea bank | Shortlist with scoring, now/later/skip status, and rationale |
| Measurement and decisions | Primary outcome; metric definitions; guardrails; cadence; decision owners; open issues |

For each AARRR section include current evidence, proposed moves, the near-term sequence, and longer-term conditions. Mark non-applicable stages explicitly with a reason.

## Idea generation and prioritization

### Generate from the constraint

Start with the audience's unmet job and the plan's limiting stage. Generate varied mechanisms, then select a shortlist rather than reproducing a catalog. Prior work, access, and capability matter more than a tactic's popularity.

| Category | Illustrative ideas | Fit or prerequisite |
|---|---|---|
| Search and reusable content | Answer a narrow buying question; refresh useful help content; repurpose research | Real intent, expertise, and maintainable content; see [seo](seo.md) |
| Alternatives and evaluation | Fair comparison; switching guide; buyer checklist | Verified comparisons and a meaningful difference |
| Useful tools and product-led discovery | Calculator; assessment; importer | Standalone usefulness, maintenance, and a relevant next step |
| Paid distribution | Intent-search test; audience-specific creative test | Approved test resources and downstream measurement |
| Social, community, and expertise | Expert Q&A; practical demonstration; useful discussion contribution | Audience presence and sustained participation; see [social](social.md), [community](community.md) |
| Lifecycle and service | Welcome guidance; post-purchase help; relevant reactivation draft | Consent, accurate product state, and a meaningful next action |
| Partnerships and referrals | Joint educational asset; complementary bundle; share-after-value prompt | Mutual audience value and feasible terms; see [loops](loops.md) |
| Events, media, and launches | Workshop; original research briefing; launch demonstration | A relevant story and a conversion/follow-through path; see [launch](launch.md) |
| Platforms and localization | Improve a discovery listing; local buyer guide | Current platform requirements and regional delivery readiness |
| Unconventional audience-specific work | Personalized diagnostic; small physical-mail concept; creative challenge | Relevance, relationship value, qualified audience, and bounded cost |

Unconventional work needs a memorable connection to the customer's problem, not spectacle alone. Prepare a small pilot with qualified recipients, a response measure, costs, and a repeatable follow-up hypothesis. Verify current platform specifications before preparing platform-specific assets.

### Rank before detailing

Apply feasibility and ethics gates first: the idea must be deliverable, supported by evidence or labelled as a hypothesis, compatible with policy, and within approved capacity. Hold a candidate with an unresolved dependency rather than scoring it as executable.

Use a consistent heuristic: **priority = impact × confidence × constraint fit / effort**. Score impact, fit, and effort from 1–5; confidence from 0.2–1 based on evidence. These are relative judgments, not forecast probabilities. Higher effort means higher total production, maintenance, and coordination cost. Explain each score briefly.

Tie-break by learning value, time-to-signal, reversibility, reusable assets, and opportunity cost. Avoid false precision: materially similar scores belong in the same tier. Re-score when evidence or resources change.

### Idea shortlist output

Return a small ranked set, normally three to five candidates, plus any important deferred or rejected option:

| Idea / AARRR stage | Why it fits / evidence | Scores and priority | First steps | Resources / dependency | Success and stop signal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Candidate | Audience mechanism and uncertainty | Impact, confidence, fit, effort | Two or three concrete steps | Owner, time, spend, readiness | Metric, observation window, guardrails |

Expand the selected idea into an experiment brief: hypothesis; audience; proposed asset or change; baseline; primary measure; quality guardrails; resource ceiling; decision date; continue/change/stop criteria; authorization needed.

## Psychology and mental models

Use these as hypotheses about decisions, not universal laws or guaranteed conversion lifts. Apply them to help people understand and choose freely. No dark patterns: fake scarcity, fabricated proof, hidden charges, obstructed cancellation, or coercive defaults.

| Principle | Decision it changes | Ethical application |
|---|---|---|
| Jobs to be done / first principles | Which problem deserves the offer or channel? | Explain the actual desired outcome and validate it with customer evidence |
| Theory of constraints / local versus global optimization | What should improve first? | Fix the limiting system step before polishing a minor surface |
| Opportunity cost / diminishing returns | Where should effort move? | Compare marginal outcomes and foregone alternatives |
| Second-order thinking / sunk cost | Should a campaign or discount continue? | Consider renewal, margin, expectations, and trust; judge future value separately from past spend |
| Probabilistic thinking / map versus territory | How certain is the recommendation? | Use ranges, customer feedback, and counterevidence alongside dashboards |
| Cognitive load / choice architecture | Why is a decision difficult? | Make options and trade-offs scannable; keep defaults transparent and reversible |
| Motivation, ability, and prompt | Why does an intended action stall? | Clarify the benefit, lower real effort, and prompt at a relevant moment |
| Social proof / authority | Why should the buyer trust the claim? | Use permissioned, relevant evidence and disclose scope or commercial relationships |
| Reciprocity / commitment | What is an appropriate next step? | Offer useful help and voluntary, proportionate next steps without implied obligation |
| Familiarity / salience | What will the buyer recognize or recall? | Maintain coherent messages and relevant repetition with consent and frequency controls |
| Framing / anchoring / contrast | What comparison helps evaluation? | Compare real alternatives, show total cost, and preserve material caveats |
| Loss aversion / status quo | What makes switching risky? | Explain real risks and switching support without fear exaggeration |
| Scarcity / timing | Is acting now genuinely useful? | State actual capacity or deadlines and what changes afterward |
| Endowment / participation | Can a buyer experience value before commitment? | Use honest samples or trials with clear data, billing, and exit terms |

Psychology output: observed barrier and evidence → selected model → proposed intervention → user benefit and autonomy check → metric and guardrail → observation window. Favor the smallest change that tests the explanation.

## Offer design

An offer is the actual exchange: deliverables, terms, support, risk, price, and customer work. Stronger wording alone cannot repair an undeliverable offer. Use the claim ledger from SKILL.md: every promised outcome, feature, timeline, bonus, price, guarantee, and capacity limit must trace to actual product or policy evidence; proposed changes remain clearly labelled pending approval and readiness.

### Diagnose value before reducing price

The value equation is a qualitative lens: **desired outcome × credible likelihood / (time delay × effort and sacrifice)**. It is not a numerical purchasing model.

1. State the existing offer in plain language: audience, deliverable, price, customer obligations, timeline, risk terms, and reason to act.
2. Assess four levers with a consistently oriented rubric: outcome relevance, confidence in delivery, speed to first value, and ease of participation. Higher scores mean better value on every lever.
3. Identify the weakest evidenced lever. More features may not solve low trust; a price cut may not solve switching effort.
4. Propose one meaningful change, identify the delivery or policy work it requires, and compare it with the unchanged offer.
5. Assess margin, support load, refund exposure, and buyer quality alongside conversion. Estimate improvement only from relevant evidence; otherwise state the test hypothesis.

### Specify components, not mandatory embellishments

| Component | Decision rule |
|---|---|
| Core | Name the outcome and mechanism, then specify inclusions, exclusions, delivery format, prerequisites, timeline, and buyer responsibilities |
| Useful extras | Include only extras that remove observed objections, accelerate value, or support implementation; account for their real delivery cost |
| Risk reversal | Address the buyer's actual risk using terms the project can honor |
| Timing or availability | Include only a real deadline, inventory limit, start date, or service capacity; an evergreen offer can have none |
| Name | Use a clear outcome or format name consistent with the brand; specificity is stronger than an invented “secret” |
| Price and payment | Show total payable amount, currency, billing schedule, renewal, fees, cancellation, and any financing cost |

Tie each extra to an objection: templates for setup effort; implementation help for time; support for getting stuck; stakeholder material for internal approval. Proof is evidence, not an inflated bonus valuation. State standalone value only when a real comparable price supports it.

### Choose risk reversal responsibly

| Buyer risk | Candidate structure | Required check |
|---|---|---|
| Fit or satisfaction uncertain | Sample, trial, or refund window | Eligibility, duration, billing, refund handling, and accessible exit |
| Delivery timing or quality | Milestone acceptance or service-level remedy | Observable deliverable, deadline, exclusions, and bounded remedy |
| Complex implementation | Scoped paid pilot or staged commitment | Success criteria, required access, handoff, and later commitment terms |
| Outcome requiring customer effort | Conditional refund or bounded extension | Fair achievable conditions, shared responsibilities, measurable outcome, and cost ceiling |
| No additional guarantee is viable | Clear existing rights and stronger evidence | Applicable legal rights remain intact; state limitations plainly |

Choose terms from delivery control, customer risk, measurement quality, and sustainable economics, not supposed audience sophistication. Avoid guaranteeing outcomes controlled by markets or third parties. Distinguish cancellation, refund, service credits, and outcome guarantees: they solve different risks.

### Match format and framing to the business

Services need clear scope, milestones, capacity, and change control. Courses or coaching need realistic learning outcomes and participant obligations. Subscriptions usually benefit from clear packaging, fast first value, and switching support more than a large bonus stack. Communities need participation and access expectations. Marketplaces need credible availability, matching, fulfillment, fees, and dispute terms on each side.

Frame benefits with the audience's actual job, show relevant proof and trade-offs, and make the full commitment easy to compare. Present a periodic equivalent only alongside total billing and renewal terms. Discounting is a choice to test against margin, cohort quality, renewal, and future price expectations, not a universally prohibited or superior tactic.

Scarcity limits quantity; urgency limits time. Link either to an enforceable operational fact and define what happens at expiry. Fake timers, invented inventory limits, and fabricated reference prices are prohibited. Genuine evergreen availability is preferable to artificial urgency.

### Offer output format

Deliver a concise specification:

- **Audience and job:** intended buyer, trigger, outcome, exclusions, and evidence.
- **Current diagnosis:** weak value lever, buyer objection, and supporting observation.
- **Proposed offer:** name, core scope, extras with objection mapping, customer work, first value, and delivery timeline.
- **Commercial terms:** total price, payment/renewal, eligibility, cancellation, refund/remedy conditions, and verified timing limits.
- **Readiness and economics:** claim/source status, product or policy dependencies, accountable owner, capacity, margin, and downside exposure.
- **Test brief:** changed component, comparison, primary outcome, retention/refund/trust guardrails, observation window, resource ceiling, and decision criteria.

Prepare the draft/checklist for changing live offers, policies, prices, or promotional messages; execution needs explicit authorization naming the action (see SKILL.md).
