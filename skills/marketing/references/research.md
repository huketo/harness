# Customer and Competitor Research

This file covers customer evidence gathering and synthesis, dated competitor profiles, and accurate comparison or alternative-page drafts.

## Customer research

### Define the decision and sample

Collect the decision to inform, target segment, existing evidence, unresolved hypotheses, relevant period, and access or consent constraints.
Choose a research mode rather than collecting material without a decision:

- **Analyze existing assets:** extract from interviews, support conversations, surveys, win/loss notes, or cancellation reasons.
- **Observe existing signal:** mine relevant reviews and community discussions for unsolicited language and actual alternatives.
- **Prepare primary research:** design interviews or surveys when existing evidence cannot resolve a specific uncertainty.

Write a hypothesis and the observation that would contradict it before extraction.
Segment by the job and context: role or household, use case, tenure, activity, buying authority, retention, geography, or accessibility needs where relevant.
Include successful, struggling, lost, and departed users when the decision concerns the whole journey; recruit retained users when specifically investigating durable value.
B2B research may distinguish user, buyer, and approver; consumer research may distinguish purchaser and user; marketplaces should examine both sides separately.

### Choose sources

| Source | Extract | Bias or limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Interviews and win/loss conversations | Decision timeline, trigger, previous attempts, desired outcome, objections, actual behavior | Recall and rationalization; recruitment favors respondents |
| Reviews, including app and product marketplaces | Job, praise, failure, unmet need, switching context, version or tier | Strong opinions, incentives, and obsolete versions |
| Support tickets | Bugs, confusion, missing capability, expectation mismatch, resolution | Represents help-seeking users and problems, not all value |
| Community threads and comments | Workarounds, recommendations, jargon, evaluation criteria, social or emotional stakes | Vocal participants; community norms shape expression |
| Surveys | Segment patterns, stated priorities, open-ended language, usage context | Question framing, nonresponse, option design, self-selection |

Find communities around the audience's activity or problem, not only around the product category.
Search problem phrases, role/activity terms, “recommend,” “alternative,” “switched,” and “how do you handle”; use site-specific searches when useful.
For reviews, sample positive, mixed, and negative experiences and recent entries; a rating is a filter, not proof of honesty.
Read the surrounding discussion to distinguish one person's experience from repeated independent reports.
A job posting signals stated hiring needs, not confirmed current behavior; audience aggregates reveal patterns, not individual motivations.
Use public or legitimately accessible material, minimize personal data, and preserve confidentiality and consent boundaries.

### Prepare interviews and surveys

1. Recruit participants whose experience matches the decision; document selection criteria and missing segments.
2. Explain the purpose honestly, obtain recording consent when needed, and keep the session conversational rather than promotional.
3. Ask for the last concrete episode: what happened, what they tried, who was involved, and how they chose.
4. Explore the trigger, alternatives including doing nothing, costs of the old approach, outcome, and switching friction.
5. Follow up with “What made that matter?” or “Can you give an example?” until the motivation is clear; avoid forcing a predetermined number of “whys.”
6. Explore what worked as well as what failed; capture valued outcomes and reasons to return or recommend.
7. Ask what would disprove the current hypothesis and note contradictions between stated preferences and reported behavior.

A compact interview guide:

- “Tell me about the last time you needed to [job].”
- “What changed that made you look for a different approach?”
- “What did you try, and what happened?”
- “What made you choose or reject an option?”
- “What would a successful result look like, and how would you know?”

For surveys, give each question a decision it supports; keep wording neutral and test for ambiguity.
Use open text for language discovery and explicit options for quantification, including “other” or “not applicable” when appropriate.
Report exact question wording, invitation population, responses, completion, collection dates, and segment denominators.
Separate prompted answers from unsolicited themes and stated intentions from demonstrated behavior.
A “How would you feel if you could no longer use this?” question can track perceived dependence among eligible active users; compare cohorts and dates rather than treating a universal threshold as proof of product-market fit.
Choose incentives and sample size for the audience, ethical constraints, and decision stakes; qualitative repetition is not statistical representativeness.

### Extract and synthesize

Jobs to be done (JTBD) describe progress in a situation, not a requested feature.
For each item, extract:

- **Situation and trigger:** what changed and why the need arose now.
- **Functional job:** task or outcome sought.
- **Emotional job:** how the person wants to feel.
- **Social job:** how they want to be perceived, where supported.
- **Pain and workaround:** what failed, its consequence, and the previous approach.
- **Desired outcome:** success criteria in the person's words.
- **Alternatives and objections:** competing products, manual work, hiring, abstaining, and barriers to change.
- **Valued experience:** what they would miss, repeat, or recommend.

Express a job as: “When [situation], I want to [progress], so I can [outcome].” Label missing elements as unknown instead of inventing them.
Preserve exact wording separately from interpretation. Use quotation marks for verbatim excerpts; show omissions and redactions explicitly.
Record provenance with every quote: source ID, URL or authorized asset location, speaker pseudonym if needed, source date, retrieval date, segment, surrounding context, and timestamp or page locator.
Translation is a separate field; keep the original wording and identify the translation. An internal quote is not permission for a public testimonial.

Synthesize in this order:

1. Deduplicate copied posts, repeated tickets for one incident, and cross-posted reviews; count independent people or sources consistently.
2. Cluster by pains, triggers, jobs, outcomes, objections, alternatives, and language while preserving segment differences.
3. Show frequency as a count and denominator within the collected sample; do not convert convenience samples into market prevalence.
4. Assess consequence and intensity from concrete stakes and language; distinguish a vivid outlier from a widespread problem.
5. Retain representative quotes, counterexamples, and differences between newer and older evidence.
6. Separate observation, interpretation, and proposed action; prioritize decision relevance, recurrence, consequence, and evidence strength.

Assign confidence with a reason, not just a label:

| Confidence | Basis |
|---|---|
| High | Relevant, recent, independently corroborated evidence with known coverage and no unresolved material contradiction |
| Medium | Repeated but narrow, prompted, proxy, or partially contradictory evidence |
| Low | Isolated, old, ambiguous, or weakly matched evidence requiring further work |

Multiple sources do not guarantee independence. First-party research is not automatically stronger than a well-matched public source; judge relevance, methods, recency, and corroboration.
For a new category, use direct alternatives and adjacent solutions as proxy evidence and label the resulting segment profile provisional.
Build segment portraits from jobs, constraints, triggers, and actual behavior; omit invented demographic detail and decorative persona biographies.

## Competitor profiling

### Collect a dated snapshot

Start from supplied URLs, the competitive question, quick-scan or deep-profile scope, and comparable market/plan/use-case boundaries.
Discover real pages from navigation or search. For a quick scan, prioritize homepage, pricing, and the capability relevant to the decision; deepen only where it changes the conclusion.

| Page or source | Collect |
|---|---|
| Homepage and product pages | Exact headline, value proposition, audience signals, CTA, capabilities, advertised proof |
| Pricing and terms | Currency, region, taxes, billing interval, usage/seat unit, tiers, limits, add-ons, commitment, free/trial conditions |
| Documentation and help | Actual workflows, prerequisites, restrictions, plan availability, support commitments |
| About and public company records | Relevant company facts with their own dates; omit unsupported size/funding estimates |
| Customer stories | Stated customer outcomes, named segments, measurement context; distinguish marketing claims from verified results |
| Compatibility pages | Supported connections, formats, requirements, and constraints relevant to the job |
| Changelog and dated archives | Recent releases and demonstrated changes, not inferred motives or unsupported velocity claims |
| Reviews and switching accounts | Praise, complaints, context, sampled dates, and representative quotes |
| Content and search evidence | Topics, audience, discovery channels, and measured search data if available |

Preserve relevant excerpts or permitted snapshots before synthesis, using source IDs and retrieval dates; retain previous snapshots when updating.
Give every claim its URL or asset locator, source date where available, retrieval date, scope, and verification status.
Keep three layers distinct: **observed** source statement, **inferred** interpretation with confidence, and **implication** as an option for the project.
“Not observed on [pages checked] as of [date]” differs from “not available.” Confirm absence with authoritative documentation or an authorized hands-on test before using it publicly.
“No public price found” does not imply free or expensive. State what changed, not why, unless the competitor explicitly supplies the reason.
Traffic, rankings, and authority metrics are provider estimates: attach provider, date, geography, device, and method; mark unavailable metrics unknown.
Search overlap is not necessarily product competition, and traffic cannot validate a claimed customer count.
Apply the same dimensions to each profile; preserve competitor strengths and the project's real limitations.

### Profile output

```text
Competitor / canonical URL / generated and last-verified dates:
Research depth / use case, plan, region, and pages checked:
Positioning: exact headline, audience signals, and labelled interpretation
Capabilities: workflows, strengths, constraints, evidence IDs
Pricing: tiers, billing/usage assumptions, inclusions, exclusions, source/date
Customer evidence: sampled praise, complaints, switching context, limitations
Discovery/content: observed topics; optional dated metric estimates
Best fit / poor fit: evidence-backed scenarios, not universal rankings
Project implications: options, confidence, and remaining verification
Evidence register: claim ID | statement | source + locator | dates | status/scope
Change log: date | old/new observation | supporting snapshots
```

For several competitors, add a landscape table using shared criteria, dated evidence IDs, unknown cells, and segment-specific recommendations.
Optional positioning maps need buyer-relevant axes and evidence for placements; apparent whitespace is a hypothesis, not proven demand.

## Comparison and alternative pages

### Select page type by reader intent

| Type | Reader's decision | Required emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| One competitor alternative | Whether to switch to the project's product | Supported switching reasons, fit, tradeoffs, migration |
| Multiple alternatives | Which options deserve evaluation | Transparent criteria, real options, same-depth summaries, use-case recommendations |
| Project vs competitor | Which of two choices fits | Summary, comparable dimensions, strengths and limitations of both |
| Competitor A vs B | How two other options differ | Answer that comparison first; disclose any relevant project option rather than forcing it |

Reuse centralized competitor profiles and evidence IDs across pages instead of maintaining independent facts in each draft.
Select dimensions from customer research: job outcomes, workflow, price at equivalent usage, usability, compatibility, service, trust requirements, or community liquidity where relevant.
Explain how each difference affects a concrete scenario; tables summarize, prose supplies the decision context.

### Fairness and accuracy rules

- Source and date every competitor claim, including table cells, pricing, strengths, limitations, and recommendations based on those facts.
- Present observable differences respectfully; no disparagement, fabricated rankings, unsupported motives, or selective omission of material strengths.
- Separate experience reports from product facts: a review complaint supports “this reviewer reported,” not a universal limitation.
- Use descriptive cells rather than unexplained checkmarks or subjective star scores. Describe verified availability, conditions, and unknowns.
- Compare equivalent plans and usage. State currency, billing basis, limits, add-ons, taxes, and scenario assumptions; avoid unsupported “hidden cost” language.
- Make “best for” conditional on the reader's requirements and disclose the page author's commercial interest.
- Include the project's limitations and reasons to stay with a competitor. Use only genuine, appropriately permitted testimonials and supported outcome claims.
- Describe migration transfers, reconfiguration, losses, effort, and assistance from evidence; distinguish a proposed process from a tested one.

### Page draft output

```text
Page type / audience / decision / target query:
Proposed URL / title / meta description:
Author-interest disclosure / last verified / next review:
1. Short answer: key differences and who each option suits
2. Reader criteria and methodology: scope, plans, source dates, limits
3. At-a-glance table: dimension | options | dated evidence references
4. Detailed dimensions: approach, strength, limitation, scenario, source
5. Fit recommendations: choose each option when; who should not switch
6. Pricing scenario: inputs, comparable total, exclusions, source dates
7. Switching path, if relevant: transfers, reconfiguration, risks, support
8. Customer evidence: permission-cleared quotes and supported results
9. Relevant CTA and links to product detail or further comparisons
Claim checklist: statement | evidence ID | verification date | unresolved issue
```

For multiple alternatives, repeat the same criteria for each genuine option and explain ordering; a self-ranked list is not independent validation.
For competitor A vs B, introduce the project's product only when it meets an explicitly identified need.
Use [seo](seo.md) for query intent and internal linking and [copy](copy.md) for the final prose; neither substitutes for factual verification.

### Maintenance and claim audit

Set an owner and review date for centralized profiles and all derived pages. A starting cadence is quarterly checks of pricing and major features, annual full reviews, and immediate checks after a reported change; adjust to market volatility.
Re-verify before publishing or republishing, and propagate corrected facts to every affected page or internal competitive asset.
Audit claims as **current**, **changed**, **unverifiable**, or **overclaimed**, with a check date and replacement wording; unchecked claims remain unchecked.
Prioritize public pages, then customer-facing scripts and decks, then internal documents. History claims require multiple dated snapshots.
Deliver an audit table: asset/claim location | old wording | status | current source/date | replacement | affected assets | next review.
