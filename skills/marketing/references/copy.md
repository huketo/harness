# Marketing copy

This file covers drafting, editing, and refreshing marketing copy that helps a defined audience understand an offer and choose a next step.

## Choose the workflow

| Need | Start here | Deliverable |
|---|---|---|
| Write new copy or change the audience, promise, or angle | [Draft new copy](#draft-new-copy) | Section-by-section copy deck |
| Improve existing copy while preserving its message | [Edit in focused passes](#edit-in-focused-passes) | Revised deck and material change notes |
| Update stale facts, examples, offers, or voice | [Refresh existing copy](#refresh-existing-copy) | Refresh deck with evidence gaps and changed details |
| Find a positioning or persuasion premise | [Strategy](strategy.md) | Settle the premise before polishing sentences |
| Obtain customer language or missing evidence | [Research](research.md) | Source-backed inputs for the deck |

For launch sequencing use [launch](launch.md); for social formats use [social](social.md); for search intent and page discovery use [SEO](seo.md).

## Inputs to collect

Use available project information first; collect only what is missing for this surface.

- Audience, their immediate task, awareness level, and relevant objections.
- Surface, traffic source, reading context, and the one primary next action.
- Offer, current availability, mechanism, meaningful difference, and approved terms.
- Customer wording, brand voice, audience language, and space constraints.
- Existing copy when editing, plus source-backed proof and known performance concerns.

Resolve uncertainty about the central promise before drafting it as fact. Missing optional proof can remain an explicit evidence gap outside publication-ready copy.

## Principles

### Clarity before cleverness

Say what the offer is, who it helps, and what changes for the reader. A memorable line earns its place when the meaning remains immediate.

- Put the main point before qualifications and background.
- Use familiar words and concrete verbs; explain necessary specialist terms.
- Keep one argument per section and one main idea per sentence.
- Use headings that communicate value when read without the body text.
- Let readers identify the offer and the next step without decoding a metaphor.

### Specificity without invented precision

Name the action, mechanism, situation, or relevant constraint instead of relying on adjectives. A concrete description is useful even when there is no approved metric.

| Weak direction | Better direction |
|---|---|
| “A powerful workflow” | State which task it performs and what the reader does next |
| “Save time” | Describe the manual step removed; quantify only with evidence |
| “Built for everyone” | Name the audience and use case supported by the offer |
| “Easy setup” | Describe the actual setup sequence and prerequisites |

Use the competitor swap test: if the line could appear unchanged on an alternative's page, add a real distinction or flag that the differentiator is unresolved.

### Customer language, not company shorthand

Use words from interviews, reviews, support conversations, or other relevant audience research. Preserve the underlying meaning rather than copying every colloquialism.

- Name the problem as readers experience it, not as an internal department labels it.
- Keep one term for one concept across the page.
- Make the reader the subject when describing their actions and outcomes.
- Match technical detail to what the audience needs to decide.

### Benefits grounded in features

Build each benefit from a supported chain:

`Capability → how it works → practical consequence → audience benefit`

Ask “Why does this matter to this reader?” until the consequence is useful. Stop before the chain turns into an unsupported promise. A capability that helps a reader act is not evidence that every reader will achieve the same result.

### One primary CTA

Choose the next action that matches the reader's intent and readiness. Repeating that action is useful; introducing competing primary actions weakens the decision.

A secondary path can serve visitors who need more information, especially on homepages. Give it less prominence and explain its role. The primary action may be purchasing, booking, joining, trying, downloading, or creating, rather than a software trial by default.

## Draft new copy

1. Write a one-sentence brief: audience, situation, supported promise, and next action.
2. Choose the page outline below; include only sections needed to make the decision.
3. Map objections and evidence to sections before writing prose.
4. Draft the hero: one core message, a clarifying subhead, primary CTA, and necessary terms.
5. Draft the body as a sequence of reader questions: relevance, mechanism, credibility, fit, commitment.
6. Add proof where it answers doubt, not merely where a layout has an empty slot.
7. Write the final CTA and expectation-setting microcopy.
8. Run the focused editing passes and produce the copy deck.

Transitions should express a real relationship: cause, example, contrast, or next step. Prefer a direct sentence or clear heading over ceremonial phrases announcing the next section.

## Page and section structures

Treat these as compact starting outlines, not mandatory page lengths. Adjust order to the audience's knowledge and the evidence available.

### Homepage

1. Hero: category or offer, broad supported value, audience fit, primary action.
2. Relevance: important use cases or clearly labelled paths for distinct visitor intents.
3. Benefits: the few consequences that matter most, linked to mechanisms.
4. Credibility: relevant proof or a concrete product demonstration.
5. Decision support: how it works, important constraints, and key objections.
6. Final action: repeat the primary CTA; retain useful secondary navigation.

Serve multiple audiences through explicit paths rather than a vague universal promise. Company history belongs only where it helps explain trust or the offer.

### Landing page

1. Hero: match the originating ad, email, referral, or search promise.
2. Situation: establish the audience's problem or desired outcome without unnecessary agitation.
3. Offer: show the mechanism and strongest relevant benefits.
4. Proof: support the main promise with suitable evidence.
5. Commitment: explain steps, eligibility, costs, terms, and likely objections.
6. Final action: repeat the same primary CTA with clear expectations.

Make the argument understandable on this page. Use supporting links when readers need detail, while keeping the primary decision clear.

### Feature or capability page

1. Hero: capability plus the specific outcome it supports.
2. Use case: who needs it, in what situation, and why.
3. Mechanism: describe or demonstrate what happens.
4. Benefit and proof: connect the capability to a supported consequence.
5. Fit: prerequisites, limits, availability, or relevant comparison.
6. Action: route readers to the next useful step.

Explain dependencies where they matter. A feature planned for later belongs in clearly labelled future-facing copy, not a present-tense capability claim.

### Announcement

1. Lead: what changed and who can use it.
2. Significance: why the change matters to that audience.
3. Demonstration: concrete use case, brief walkthrough, or before/after workflow.
4. Details: availability date, eligibility, access method, and important limits.
5. Action: try the change, read details, register interest, or another appropriate next step.

Use an offer deadline or scarcity only when it is a real, documented condition. Coordinate distribution through [launch](launch.md).

### Reusable section units

| Section | Minimum useful content |
|---|---|
| Hero | Core message; subhead supplying mechanism or fit; CTA; necessary microcopy |
| Benefit | Outcome heading; explanation of how; relevant evidence when available |
| How it works | Real steps, each with an action and result; prerequisites where needed |
| Proof | Evidence, attribution, context, and the exact claim it supports |
| Comparison | Audience-relevant criteria, dated evidence, and fair scope |
| FAQ | Genuine decision-blocking question and direct, supported answer |
| Final CTA | Concise value recap, same primary action, and commitment expectations |

## Headline and CTA patterns

### Headlines

Use patterns as thinking prompts, then rewrite them naturally for the audience. Select the angle that explains the offer most accurately.

| Angle | Drafting pattern | Evidence check |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome and mechanism | `[Achieve outcome] with [specific mechanism]` | Both outcome and mechanism are supported |
| Category and audience | `[Offer/category] for [audience and task]` | Audience fit is meaningful, not decorative |
| Transformation | `Turn [input/current state] into [useful result]` | The product actually enables the transformation |
| Difference | `[Category] that [distinct capability]` | Distinction is real and relevant |
| Proof | `[Verified result] in [defined context]` | Measurement, scope, and date support the wording |

Use a time-bound outcome only when the evidence supports the time window. Absolute promises such as “always” or “never” require evidence adequate to that scope.

Keep the headline focused; let the subhead add the mechanism, audience detail, or condition. Compare a small set of materially different angles rather than many synonym variations.

### CTAs and microcopy

Use an action verb plus the object or next step: “Download the checklist,” “Book a consultation,” “Create your first project,” or “Join the community.” These are generic patterns, not claims about the project.

- Name what the destination actually does; a purchase button should not imply a preview.
- Match commitment to the reader's readiness and the page's purpose.
- Explain what follows a form submission or click when that is not obvious.
- Put important price, eligibility, trial, cancellation, or privacy terms near the decision.
- Use precise secondary labels such as “Read the example” when that is the destination.
- Keep short labels recognisable and readable; “Submit” may be appropriate for a genuine submission task, but not when a clearer object is available.

Prepare the draft/checklist; publishing, sending, posting, or changing live copy needs explicit authorization naming the action (see SKILL.md).

## Edit in focused passes

Read the whole draft once before changing it. Identify the intended promise, action, and author's voice. Preserve those unless the assignment calls for a strategic rewrite.

For substantial edits, use these seven passes in order. For a small correction, use the affected passes plus claims and final checks. After each substantive change, revisit earlier passes it could affect.

| Pass | Decision to make | Typical edit |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Clarity | Can a reader understand each sentence on first reading? | Resolve pronouns, split overloaded sentences, surface missing context |
| 2. Voice | Does the whole surface sound like the same brand addressing this audience? | Align register, terminology, person, and appropriate emotional intensity |
| 3. Relevance | Does each feature or claim explain why the reader should care? | Add the supported practical consequence; cut irrelevant company-centred detail |
| 4. Evidence | Does the wording match the strength and scope of its source? | Correct or remove unsupported assertions; attach relevant proof |
| 5. Specificity | Could a concrete action, mechanism, or example replace vague praise? | Replace abstractions with sourced detail, without manufacturing numbers |
| 6. Resonance | Does the situation reflect a real concern or aspiration? | Use recognisable context and authentic emotion rather than inflated fear |
| 7. Commitment | Is the next step clear, with material concerns addressed? | Clarify costs, terms, prerequisites, and what happens next |

The commitment pass reduces uncertainty; it does not promise zero risk. A guarantee is an actual policy, not a device to add whenever a CTA feels weak.

### Final language checks

For Korean, English or Japanese copy, also apply the Common section of the matching style reference: [Korean](../../writing-for-humans/references/style-ko.md), [English](../../writing-for-humans/references/style-en.md), [Japanese](../../writing-for-humans/references/style-ja.md).

- Prefer verbs to bulky noun phrases: “decide” rather than “make a decision.”
- Use simpler wording where meaning survives: “use,” “help,” “start,” and “because.”
- Cut throat-clearing, empty intensifiers, repeated claims, and decorative transitions.
- State the fact directly instead of staging a reveal such as “It's not X, it's Y.”
- Describe what happens instead of stacking “no X, no Y” claims.
- End complete claims cleanly; give a distinct benefit its own sentence when needed.
- Keep real FAQ questions; replace self-answered promotional questions with the answer.
- Vary sentence rhythm naturally, with formatting driven by content rather than repeated rhetorical patterns.
- Read aloud for forced language; read headings and CTAs alone for coherence.
- Check grammar, spelling, terminology, link destinations, and layout constraints.

For high-stakes surfaces, prepare a review checklist from the reader, brand, and evidence perspectives. Record concrete issues and edits rather than treating simulated reviewer scores as validation.

## Refresh existing copy

Refresh when product details, prices, proof, audience language, or positioning have changed; performance decline is a reason to investigate, not proof that wording is the cause.

| What changed | Response |
|---|---|
| Details or links; central promise still valid | Targeted factual refresh |
| Voice; audience and promise still valid | Refresh plus voice pass |
| Audience, offer, angle, or central promise | Rewrite from the brief |
| Search intent or page structure | Reassess outline with [SEO](seo.md), then rewrite affected sections |

Inventory dated claims and volatile details first. Check current availability, prices, policies, examples, evidence, and destinations; replace outdated material with supported current wording. Re-run relevant editing passes.

Set review timing by volatility and importance, with event-driven review when the offer changes. A visible “updated” date should reflect a substantive review, not a cosmetic date change. For evaluating results, use [measurement](measurement.md).

## Proof and claims

Use the claim ledger from SKILL.md for every product claim and every proof element: numbers, testimonials, logos, review scores, comparisons, guarantees, and offer terms. Every element must trace to a project source with its status and date; never invent proof or turn an illustrative pattern into a factual assertion.

- Numbers retain units, population, measurement period, and important conditions.
- Testimonials retain authentic wording, attribution, context, and approved usage; edits must preserve meaning.
- Logos establish only the documented relationship and permitted use, not an implied endorsement.
- Guarantees and risk-reversal copy match actual policy, exclusions, and eligibility.
- Comparative and superlative claims require evidence matching the comparison and scope.
- Screenshots and demonstrations reflect the actual available experience; label prototypes or planned functionality accurately.

If evidence is missing, remove the assertion or use a narrower statement that has support. Merely adding “may” does not substantiate an invented benefit. Record unresolved evidence in review notes, outside publishable copy. Source numbers never become universal market benchmarks by reuse.

## Writing in the audience's language

Transcreate the intent and decision logic rather than translating English formulas word for word. Choose natural phrasing for the audience's culture, familiarity, and buying context.

- Keep register consistent with the brand voice. For Korean, choose 해요체 or 합쇼체 per surface and hold it; headings and button labels can follow the surface's agreed conventions.
- Replace literal English idioms, metaphors, and imported rhetorical rhythms with locally natural expressions.
- Use audience-native vocabulary and local conventions for dates, currency, units, names, and address forms.
- Preserve factual scope, qualifications, and offer terms across languages; explain unfamiliar concepts rather than weakening the claim boundary.
- For CJK copy, count characters for length limits rather than English word counts; verify the destination's current character-count rules and specification before use.
- Check the actual display space, line breaks, CTA readability, and whether expansion or truncation changes meaning.

## Output: copy deck

Start with a short brief stating the surface, audience, language/register, traffic context, promise, and primary CTA. Separate finished copy from rationale and review notes.

| Section | Copy | Rationale | Claims used |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hero headline | Proposed headline | Angle and audience relevance | Ledger IDs, status/date, or “None” |
| Hero subhead | Proposed subhead | Mechanism, fit, or qualification | Relevant ledger IDs and scope |
| Benefit / proof / FAQ | Complete section text | Reader question or objection answered | Ledger IDs for claims and proof |
| Primary CTA + microcopy | Label and next-step explanation | Commitment and destination | Ledger IDs for offer terms, or “None” |

For variants, label the hypothesis and keep the claim boundary unchanged. For edits, add a compact change log: original excerpt, revision, and reason for material changes. For refreshes, list changed facts and their dated sources.

Close with unresolved inputs or evidence gaps, relevant layout/spec checks, and the authorization-required action. A draft with unresolved claims is reviewable, not publication-ready.
